Track II · The data engine · ~8 min

Entity resolution — the spine.

Ask "who files the most patents?" and the raw data lies to you. Fixing that lie is the single biggest credibility win in the product.

The problem

The raw data doesn't know what a company is.

Patent offices record whatever an applicant writes in the assignee field when they file. There is no global registry, no canonical company ID, no enforcement of consistent naming. The result: the same real company shows up as a dozen or more different strings across the corpus.

Take one of the world's largest patent filers. In the raw data, what should be a single entity appears as approximately ~15 different assignee strings — subsidiaries, suffix variants, spelling variants, and native-script names. For example:

Counted naively, a company that belongs in the #2 spot on a global leaderboard scatters into a dozen also-rans — none of them dominant enough to catch the eye. The ranking is wrong. The insight is invisible.

There is a second, subtler problem: banks frequently appear in patent filings as collateral agents — lenders who hold a security interest in a patent portfolio as loan collateral. Their names land in the assignee field right alongside genuine inventors and corporate owners. Counted naively, a major bank appears to be a prolific patent filer when in fact it holds zero patents as an innovator. Without flagging and filtering these records, ownership rankings are polluted.

Entity resolution is the process of deciding which records refer to the same real-world entity. In this context: mapping every assignee string to one canonical company (or flagging it as a non-owner). It is the foundational data-quality step that makes every company-level number in the product trustworthy.

Step A

Normalize the strings. Cheap, deterministic, catches the easy cases.

The first pass is purely mechanical: strip company suffixes, punctuation, and casing. The same company often differs only in whether it wrote "CO LTD" vs "Co., Ltd." vs nothing at all. A short list of rules catches a large fraction of duplicates for free — no AI required, no external lookup.

The rules applied in order:

  1. Lowercase everything.
  2. Strip legal suffixes: CO, LTD, INC, CORP, GMBH, KK, LLC, PLC, SA, SPA, BV, AG, AB and their dot variants.
  3. Remove all punctuation (commas, periods, parentheses, hyphens).
  4. Collapse whitespace; trim.

Here is a before-and-after for the Samsung example:

Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd.
SAMSUNG ELECTRONICS INC
→
After normalization (all three)

Three different raw strings collapse to one canonical form. Across the full corpus this step alone reconciles a substantial share of easy duplicates — suffixes and punctuation are the most common source of variation. But it is not sufficient on its own: a subsidiary like "Samsung Display" normalizes to "samsung display", not "samsung electronics". Step B handles the hierarchical parent–child relationship.

Step B

Roll up to the ultimate parent. Subsidiaries should count toward the parent.

Normalization handles spelling. But "Samsung Display Co., Ltd." and "Samsung Electro-Mechanics Co., Ltd." are genuinely different legal entities — they will normalize to different strings. Yet for the purpose of "how many patents does Samsung file?", they should count toward Samsung Electronics. The question is not about legal entities; it is about consolidated companies.

This is where GLEIF comes in. GLEIF — the Global Legal Entity Identifier Foundation — maintains a global registry of Legal Entity Identifiers (LEIs): unique codes that identify companies worldwide. More importantly for our purposes, it publishes "ultimately consolidated by" edges: for each entity, which entity is at the top of the ownership chain.

We built a name-to-ultimate-parent map from these edges — approximately ~129,818 ultimate-parent edges constructed and verified, with the Samsung rollup confirmed. The map gives us: subsidiary name → ultimate parent canonical name.

After GLEIF rollup, all four subsidiary strings resolve to the same ultimate parent node. Every patent filed by any of them is credited to Samsung Electronics in the analytics cube. The leaderboard now reflects economic reality, not filing-office bureaucracy.

The GLEIF rollup is particularly powerful because it handles corporate structure changes — spin-offs, acquisitions, reorganizations — as long as the registry is kept current. It is a maintained, authoritative dataset, not a hand-curated list that drifts stale.

Step C

The hard cases — a proposer–skeptic pass.

Normalization handles spelling variants. GLEIF handles known subsidiaries. But a long tail of names remains: typos, abbreviations, transliterations, historical names, holding companies with opaque names. For these, neither rule nor registry gives a confident answer.

For this residual set we run a two-agent pass:

💡
Agent 1 — Proposer

Suggests a canonical entity.

Given the ambiguous assignee string, the Proposer generates a hypothesis: "this looks like it refers to X." It draws on any contextual signal available — co-filing patterns, known parent names, common abbreviations.

🔍
Agent 2 — Skeptic

Tries to reject the proposal.

The Skeptic — a different model, deliberately chosen to be heterogeneous — evaluates the Proposer's suggestion and actively looks for reasons it is wrong. Could this name be a different company entirely? Is the evidence actually ambiguous? If the Skeptic can't definitively reject the merge, it says so.

⚠️
Uncertain → safe fallback

When in doubt, leave it as-is.

If the Proposer and Skeptic can't reach a confident answer, the record falls back to its normalized string — no merge is made. This is the deliberate, conservative default.

The bias is intentional: never merge two genuinely different companies. A missed merge is recoverable — you can discover it later and fix it. A wrong merge silently corrupts counts, and you may never know the leaderboard was lying.

The heterogeneity of Proposer and Skeptic matters. Using two instances of the same model tends to produce agreement by construction — both models share the same biases and blind spots. A different model as Skeptic genuinely challenges the proposal rather than just ratifying it.

Why it matters

Every company-level number in the cube rides on this map.

Return to the cube from D3: it pre-aggregates measures across dimensions. One of those dimensions is company. If the company dimension is wrong — if Samsung's patents scatter across fifteen bins instead of one — then every leaderboard, every trend chart, every "top filers by year" query returns wrong numbers. The cube looks authoritative. The numbers are fast. And they are wrong.

That is worse than no analytics. A slow query that produces a correct number at least reveals the truth. A fast pre-computed answer that looks confident while being wrong misleads every decision that depends on it. Entity resolution is what makes the cube's company numbers trustworthy.

This is why entity resolution is "the spine": it is not a feature of the analytics layer. It is a precondition for the analytics layer being worth anything at all.

One sentence on scope: the entity map built here is also the join point for future non-patent signals — financial disclosures, R&D spend data, trade-secret proxies. Those signals are indexed by company; entity resolution is how they connect to the same canonical entities. That's a broader topic; the important point here is that getting the map right once pays dividends across every future data layer.
