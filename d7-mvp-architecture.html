What this thing actually is.

It's a search-and-statistics engine for the world's patents — about 148.3 million of them, from the US, Europe, Japan, China and elsewhere. Customers are companies, investors and patent lawyers, and they ask it questions like:

  • “What is Samsung patenting in battery tech, and is that going up or down?”
  • “Who else is working in this area, and who's the biggest?”
  • “Has anyone already patented my idea?” prior art
  • “Is this one patent strong or weak compared to similar ones?”

Two things make it more than a patent database. First, it answers as companies, not as filing names — it knows the fifteen ways “Samsung” appears are one company. Second, patents are only signal #1; company financial filings are signal #2, with more to come. That's the whole product. Everything below is how it's built.

Words used on this page

The vocabulary, up front.

These come up constantly below. Read them once here and the rest of the page reads normally. Nothing here is standard industry vocabulary — most of it is specific to how we build.

artifact
A finished data file the API reads to answer questions. Built once on a big rented machine, then copied to the server that's always on. Most of this page is about which artifact answers what.
the cube
The biggest artifact. It holds pre-counted answers — “Samsung, batteries, Germany, 2019 → 412 patents” — so a question that would take minutes to count becomes an instant lookup.
sidecar
⚠ Two meanings on this page. Usually: a small artifact beside the main ones answering one narrow thing (patent text, examiner stats) — if it's missing, only its own feature stops. Occasionally: a separate small program running next to the API, which is what the search engine is.
ultimate parent
parent_key
The one company we roll everything up to. “SAMSUNG ELECTRONICS CO., LTD.”, “Samsung Elec” and a dozen subsidiaries all become one parent_key.
re-key
Rebuilding an artifact so its rows are grouped by a different key — by company instead of by patent, say. The catch: if the cube changes how it groups companies and a re-keyed copy isn't rebuilt too, the two disagree. That's the trap Part B is about.
degrade · 503
When an artifact is missing, that endpoint returns HTTP 503 (“this isn't available”) rather than an empty answer. Saying “I don't have this” and saying “there is none” are different claims, and we never confuse them.
decile
A 1-to-10 ranking band. A patent in the 9th decile scored higher than 80–90% of comparable patents — always compared within its own country, technology and year, never against everything at once.
prior art
Anything published before your filing date that already describes your idea. Finding it can invalidate a patent — which is why the date cut-off matters so much.
facet
A breakdown — “group these patents by year”, “by country”, “by owner”. A crosstab is the two-dimensional version: technology down the side, year across the top.
keyword search
BM25 · Tantivy
Classic word-matching search, ranked by relevance. BM25 is the ranking formula; Tantivy is the search engine we run.
search by meaning
semantic · vectors
Matching on meaning rather than words, so “self-healing polymer” can find “autonomously repairing elastomer”. Semantic search does this by turning text into numbers.
fusing two searches
RRF
Running keyword and meaning search separately, then merging the two ranked lists into one. “Reciprocal-rank fusion” is just the merging rule — no scores to calibrate, so the same inputs always give the same output.
capability
A named thing the server publishes at start-up saying “I can do this” — e.g. keyword-search, strength. The app reads that list and greys out what isn't available, instead of guessing. 20 are live today; 1 is off on purpose.
cohort
The comparison group a number is measured against. Patent strength uses “same country, same technology, same year” as the cohort — so a score always means “compared with its own peers”, never “compared with everything”.
corpus
Our full cleaned-up collection of patents — all 148,310,457 of them (~148.3M). It gets rebuilt periodically, so you'll see it talked about as having versions.
report mart
An artifact holding finished reports rather than raw data — the answers are computed during the build, so serving one is just reading a row.
bdds
Our command-line tool — it runs every build (bdds build-cube) and every server operation. ⚠ Unrelated to “EPO BDDS” in the diagrams, which is the European Patent Office's data service. Same letters, different thing.
egress
Data leaving a cloud provider's network — which they charge for. It's why publishing a huge finished file can cost more than the machine that built it.
ticket · epic · milestone
A ticket (#204) is one unit of work on GitHub. An epic is a big ticket holding smaller ones. M1–M12 are milestones — themed buckets like “M2 · Entity Resolution”. A count like “8 closed / 5 open” means 8 done, 5 to go.
EDGAR · SEC filings
The US regulator's public database of company financial reports. We read R&D spending out of it to compare what a company says it invests against what it patents.

How the statuses below were decided — and why not from the ticket list. Tickets here often stay open after the thing they describe is already working (#522, #452 and #372 are all open tickets for features that are live right now). So “Live” on this page means the running server actually reports it — checked against the server's own start-up list of what it can do, its route table, its published API contract, and the 2026-08-21 status handoff: 20 capabilities live, 1 switched off on purpose. Ticket numbers are listed for what is still missing, which is a different question from what is still open.

1

Company identity — one company, many names.

Live · v1ultimate-parent spine shipped 2026-08-27 · v2 registry map still ahead

The problem: patent offices record whatever name was typed on the form. One company shows up as dozens of strings, plus subsidiaries that don't share a name at all.

What this does: entity resolution decides which of those strings are the same company and folds them into one ultimate parent. Every count in the product is then per-company rather than per-filing-name.

Why it's called the spine: every other feature inherits this answer. Get a company wrong here and every chart, score and profile is wrong the same way.

What just shipped (2026-08-27): the cube was re-keyed to ultimate-parent resolution and is now live in production. Filing names now roll up to the real top company — “PANASONIC” and its subsidiaries fold into PANASONIC HOLDINGS, which takes it from 3,461 patents to about 1.1 million. Chinese, Japanese and Korean (CJK) name coverage went from 0% to 16.9%, and roughly 10,600 newly-resolved companies now roll up correctly. The cutover shipped as one co-fresh batch — see Part B for why it had to.

Serving now
/v1/entities/resolve, /v1/entities/suggest — FTS5 typeahead over ~25M parents, plus the parent_key dimension baked into every cube rollup.
Artifacts
entity_dim.sqlite (derived from the cube), cube_parent_map / cube_entity inside cube.duckdb, assignee_map.
Capability
entity-resolve — live.
What v1 is
Clean up the names, apply a hand-maintained list of known corrections, and bake the result into the cube when it's built. Not clever, but it works — and it is what the entire product runs on today.
What v2 adds
A real ownership map built from official company registries — GLEIF (the global registry of legal entities), Wikidata, and the Japanese and Korean equivalents — which know who actually owns whom. Today the name-matching is the truth; in v2 the registries are the truth and the name-matching becomes a copy of them.

Patent lookup — everything about one patent.

Live

What a user gets: paste a patent number and get the record — its text, the other countries it was filed in (its “family”), what it cites and what cites it, its back-and-forth with the patent office, and who has owned it over time.

Why it's fast: these are single-row lookups, so they run on SQLite files — milliseconds each. DuckDB builds those files but never serves them; it's built for scanning millions of rows, not for fetching one.

Serving now
13 routes across the patent record, the citation graph, full text, prosecution, examiner/art-unit stats and assignments.
Artifacts
lookup_lean.db (required — it is the list of every patent we hold, so it's what decides whether a number is unknown or merely missing detail), patent_detail.sqlite, patent_text.sqlite, prosecution.sqlite, oa_timeline.sqlite, examiner.sqlite, assignment.v4.sqlite, app_bridge.sqlite.
Capabilities
lean-lookup · detail-full · patent-text · prosecution · examiner · assignment-chain-of-title · case — all live.

An optional extra compression step was tried, measured at a 9.8% saving against a 15% bar, and switched off — and it records that it was switched off after measuring, not that it saved nothing. Those are different facts and the system keeps them apart.

Keyword & company search — the two search boxes.

Liveexcept searching the long description text

Two boxes, two jobs. One searches patents by words — ranked by relevance, with a simple mode and an expert mode that accepts operators like AND and wildcards. The other is the company picker: you type “samsng electr” and it finds the right company anyway.

The company picker matters more than it looks — it is the front door to nearly every other feature, because almost everything is asked about a company.

Serving now
/v1/search, /v1/search/npl + /npl/{work_id} (scholarly prior art), /v1/entities/resolve, /v1/entities/suggest (score + matched_on basis).
Artifacts
Tantivy index (308.9 GiB), the NPL Tantivy index + npl_lookup.sqlite, entity_dim.sqlite.
Capabilities
keyword-search · npl-search · entity-resolve live. description-search is the one feature switched off on purpose: today only titles, abstracts and claims are searchable, not the long description section. It turns itself on once descriptions are indexed — and until then the API says so out loud, so the app can grey out the option instead of guessing.
The disk reality
The search index is 309 GB and there is about 309 GB of free disk — roughly 0.05% to spare. Replacing it only works because the old copy is deleted before the new one downloads; there is no room to hold both at once. Worth knowing before anyone proposes “just build a second index”.

5

Prior-art search — “has anyone patented this already?”

In progress

The question: before filing, you need to know whether your idea is already public. Anything published before your filing date that describes it can sink the patent.

The approach: run two searches — one on words, one on meaning (semantic search, which catches a patent that describes your idea in completely different vocabulary) — then merge the two ranked lists into one, and filter out anything published after the cut-off date.

Where it stands: the word half works today. The meaning half is wired up but not yet switched on for prior-art, which is why this is “in progress” rather than done.

Serving now
/v1/semantic-search (incl. ?similar_to=), /v1/hybrid-search, /v1/prior-art — all three live and in the published API contract. /v1/prior-art answers today in its keyword/basic form: the merge logic is wired and the endpoint is fully typed, but the meaning-based signal is not yet lit (#196). Search-by-meaning is the fast-follow enhancement, not a separate feature.
Artifacts
IVF-PQ vector index (in-process ANN), Tantivy, lookup_lean.db for the date cutoff.
Capability
vector-index live; embedder live — the search-by-meaning machinery is loaded. If it were ever missing, hybrid search drops to keyword-only and says so in its response. It never quietly returns worse results while looking normal.
Known defect
#749 — the date cut-off currently works to the year, not the day. The exact dates never made it into the deployed lookup file. For prior art that matters: two patents in the same year can fall on opposite sides of a filing date. This is the one to fix first.

6

Strategy statistics & patent strength — the reports competitors sell.

Live
One minute of patent-office background, because this section needs it. You file an application. An examiner — a specialist civil servant — is assigned, working in an art unit (a team covering one technology). They send back office actions: written objections you must answer. Eventually the application is granted or abandoned; that outcome is its disposition, and the share that succeed is the allowance rate. “Entity size” is a legal category (large / small / micro) that sets your fees — it is not company revenue.

The reports: who is filing what — sliced by company size, applicant type, country and technology field; how often applications actually succeed; how concentrated a field is (a few giants or many small players — measured by HHI, a standard economics score where higher means more dominated by a few); and why applications get rejected, including the US eligibility test known as §101 / Alice after the 2014 Supreme Court case that made many software patents much harder to obtain.

Patent strength: a 1–10 score per patent. The important part is what it's compared against — only patents from the same country, same technology and same year. A 1998 German chemical patent is never scored against a 2021 US software one.

Serving now
14 /v1/analytics/strategic/* routes, 4 /v1/analytics/ fieldset routes (whitespace / dynamics / payoff / catalog), /v1/analytics/clusters, /v1/analytics/emerging*, and both strength routes.
Artifacts
strategic_analytics.sqlite, fx_analytics.sqlite, clusters.sqlite (16,384 IVF clusters + PCA-2D), emergingtech.sqlite, strength.sqlite.
Capabilities
strategic-analytics · fieldset-analytics · clusters-landscape · emergence-signals · strength — all live.
Honest by construction
Every row carries how many records it is based on, the exact rule used, and what it does not cover. Nothing is recalculated when serving — the number you see is the number that was computed and stamped. If an input is missing, that one report is marked unavailable rather than quietly returning a thinner answer. This is the actual selling point: competitors give you a number, we give you a number you can check.

7

Company financial signals — what they say vs what they file.

In progressone lane of eight built

The idea: patents are only one clue about what a company is really working on. US public companies also file annual reports (10-Ks) with the regulator, which state what they spend on R&D and describe what they're building.

Put the two side by side and the gap is informative: a company spending heavily on R&D but filing few patents may be deliberately keeping its work secret rather than patenting it. That gap is the signal.

Serving now
/v1/entities/{parent_key}/signals — disclosed-R&D series + intensity benchmark for a consolidated parent.
Artifacts
edgar_rd.sqlite, built from edgar_fsds + edgar_10k.
Capability
edgar-signals — live.
What's missing
The R&D spending numbers work. What doesn't yet: reliably matching a company's regulator ID to its patent identity (so we can't yet say how much of the market we cover), reading meaning out of the report text, computing the say-versus-file gap itself, and the Japanese and Korean equivalents of EDGAR.

Litigation / court-docket signal.

Cut from MVP

What it would be: who is suing whom over patents, per company — pulled from free public US court records and joined to our company identities. Cheap to build and clearly on-strategy, but deliberately not in the first release.

Code state
The URL exists and the capability is defined, so the shape is reserved for later. The data file behind it was never built and is not on the server.
Live behaviour
It returns 503 — “not available”, never an empty list. This is the clearest example of the rule from the vocabulary above: “this company has no lawsuits” and “we don't have lawsuit data” are completely different answers, and only one of them is about the company. Returning an empty list would be a lie.
Source registered

To un-cut it

Part B

What depends on what.

Data flows one way, left to right, and never loops back. Raw feeds land in cloud storage; one cleanup pass produces the corpus; the cube is built from that; and almost everything else is built from the cube. Read one column at a time.

One word first, because the next three diagrams depend on it: re-key. Several files are built by taking the cube and re-grouping its rows around a different key — by company instead of by patent, say, so a lookup gets faster. They are copies with a different filing order. The catch: if the cube is rebuilt and changes how it groups companies, those copies still hold the old grouping. Nothing errors. The two just quietly disagree. That is the failure this section is about.

The dependency that bit us

Three serving artifacts are re-keys of the cube. entity_dim.sqlite, company_file.sqlite and facet_leaderboard.sqlite are all derived from cube.duckdb. Rebuild the cube and deploy it without rebuilding them, and each one keeps serving counts for the previous corpus — with no error, no 503, and no visible symptom except two numbers on the same screen disagreeing.

That is how we actually found it: two screens disagreed. The company lookup said Novartis held 17 more patents than the technology breakdown did. Neither screen showed an error — one file had simply been rebuilt and the other had not. A stale file does not look broken. It looks like a bug in whichever number you trust less.

The fix has two parts. First, a reconciliation gate per re-key, run at deploy time against a marquee value that must be equal on both sides (below). Second, and wider: a co-fresh manifest. Parent identity is baked into more than just the three re-keys — lookup_lean.db carries each patent's ultimate parent, the strength scores and the report marts are all built from the same corpus snapshot — so a cube re-key forces the whole serving set to be rebuilt and shipped together, from one snapshot, never piecemeal. Below is what each gate compares and what happens when it fails; further down is the cutover that just ran the whole thing for real.

Take away: after the cube is rebuilt, three automatic checks ask whether its re-keyed copies still agree with it. One is cheap enough to fix itself on the spot; the other two are too expensive to rebuild on a live server, so they stop the deploy and wait for a human.

The three gates behave differently on purpose, and the reason is cost. Rebuilding the company name index is cheap — it is just a searchable list of company names, a few minutes and modest memory — so the live server can redo it itself and carry on.

The other two need full passes over 170–200 million rows. Doing that on the live server would starve real user requests of memory and disk speed while it ran. So instead of fixing themselves they stop the deploy, and a person rebuilds them on a rented machine. This is one instance of the rule that shapes the whole system: never run a heavy build on the machine that is serving users.

Proven in production · 2026-08-27

This is no longer a cautionary tale — it is the routine that just ran. The entity-spine cutover re-keyed the cube to ultimate-parent resolution, and because parent identity is baked into far more than the three re-keys, all eight serving artifacts were rebuilt and deployed as one co-fresh batch: the cube, lookup_lean.db, strength.sqlite, the facet index, the analytics marts, company_file.sqlite, facet_leaderboard.sqlite and entity_dim.sqlite. Every reconciliation gate passed, 61 smoke checks came back green, and there were zero 5xx errors.

The payoff shows up on every screen at once, because nothing was left on the old keying: Panasonic Holdings went from 3,461 to about 1.1 million patents (its subsidiaries now roll up), CJK name coverage went from 0% to 16.9%, and roughly 10,600 newly-resolved companies appeared. A fast-follow, #792, tracks about 30 residual matcher errors where legal-form suffixes collide — logged, bounded, and not blocking.

Take away: rent a big machine only for the hours a build takes, then destroy it; serve forever from a cheap one. Note the asymmetry on the arrows — downloading from storage is free, uploading to it is not, and on a large file the upload can cost five times the machine.

The egress trap. "R2 egress is free" is true when pulling: a build box can stream terabytes out of R2 for nothing. Pushing the finished artifact out of the build box's own cloud is billed by that provider, and on a large artifact it dominates the run — measured on the 309 GB search index: ~$21 egress against ~$4 for the box itself. bdds ops fire prints the projected publish egress before you spend it.

Small print: the published API spec is not a complete description of the server
Contract drift

The published openapi.json declares 72 /v1 paths. The router registers 73 paths / 74 method+path pairs. The gap is exactly the two key mutations above: POST /v1/admin/keys (an undeclared method on a declared path) and DELETE /v1/admin/keys/{id} (an undeclared path outright). Both are admin-only and gated behind Caddy, so nothing public is undeclared — but the contract is not a complete description of the router.

Separately, #770 is open: the contract under-declares response fields by 73 (56 company-file · 12 hybrid-search · 4 emerging · 1 prior-art), and bdds contract verify exits 1 on it today. The fix direction is settled — derive the contract from the Go types by reflection rather than maintaining a parallel copy (#352, the system-catalog epic, is the durable version of this).

Where the surface is public. Cloudflare → Caddy gates api.tap-ip.com down to /v1, /health and /openapi.json. The /docs Swagger UI, /ui, /admin and /jobs pages are auth-exempt and inject the API key into the page — which is precisely why they stay box-private and are reached over an SSH tunnel, never exposed.

Three things to take away

If you remember nothing else.

1 · One artifact is the keying authority. The cube decides what a company is, and three other serving artifacts are re-keys of it. Any change to how the cube keys parents desynchronises all three, silently. That is why there is a reconciliation gate per re-key, why one of them self-heals while two refuse the deploy, and why a cube re-key ships as a co-fresh batch of the whole serving set. The 2026-08-27 spine cutover ran exactly this — all eight artifacts rebuilt together, gates green, zero 5xx.

2 · Absent is not zero. Every degraded route returns a typed 503 and declares itself on /v1/capabilities. An empty 200 would be a claim about the world; a 503 is a claim about the deployment. The litigation endpoint is the clearest example — cut from the MVP, and honest about it.

3 · Status lives in the running system, not the tracker. Epics stay open after their surface ships. When you need to know whether something works, read /v1/capabilities, the route table, and the capability registry — then go look at the tickets to find out what is still missing.
