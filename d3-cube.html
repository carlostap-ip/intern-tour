The cube — pre-computing the answers.

The single most important idea in the analytics engine — and the one most worth understanding. A cube turns a slow question into an instant lookup.

The problem

Aggregates over ~147 million rows are slow.

Consider a question that every analytics dashboard wants to answer: "How many patents did each company file, per country, per year?" Sounds reasonable. But answering it against a raw table of ~147 million records means scanning every single row, reading at minimum the company, country, and year columns, and then grouping and counting — on every dashboard load, every time a user changes a filter, every time a chart redraws.

This style of question is called an OLAP query — Online Analytical Processing. The defining feature is that it works across the whole corpus at once, not just one row. You're not asking "give me patent #X." You're asking "sum up everything, group it by these three axes, give me a leaderboard." The answer involves touching millions of records even when the final result is a table of a few hundred rows.

At small scale — thousands of rows — a database handles this fine. At tens or hundreds of millions of rows, a naive aggregate query becomes a problem. A leaderboard that should snap open in under a second can take many seconds or more, and runs that slow query again every single time someone loads the page. Dashboards become sluggish. Scale up the corpus or add more concurrent users and it gets worse, not better.

There's a better approach — but it requires thinking about queries differently.

The idea

Compute the answer once. Store it. Look it up later.

The key insight: if the same expensive aggregate is going to be asked over and over, stop recomputing it every time. Run the GROUP BY once at build time, store the result, and serve it as a direct lookup. That's the entire idea behind a cube.

A cube is built from three concepts, worth defining clearly before going further:

📐
Concept 1

Dimensions — the "slice-by" axes.

A dimension is a column you filter or group on. In our patent corpus: country (where was the patent filed?), year (when?), company (who filed it?), technology area (what IPC class?). Dimensions are the things you put on the axes of a chart or use in a WHERE clause.

🔢
Concept 2

Measures — the numbers you aggregate.

A measure is what you're summing or counting. Patent count, citation count, forward-citation count. Where dimensions answer "grouped by what?", measures answer "aggregated into what number?" A dashboard cell like "Company X filed N patents in 2023 in the US" — that patent count is the measure; the company, the year, and the country are the dimensions.

⚡
Concept 3 — the core trick

Pre-aggregation — paying the cost once, at build time.

Instead of running GROUP BY on the fly at query time, run it ahead of time during the build step. Store the resulting totals. When the dashboard asks "patents per company per year," it reads the pre-computed row — one fast lookup — rather than scanning millions of records. The expensive work already happened; the query just fetches the answer.

A cube, then, is a pre-aggregated table: all your measures, pre-summed across every combination of dimensions people care about, computed once at build time and stored ready-to-read. The name "cube" comes from visualizing it as a multi-dimensional space — one axis per dimension — where each cell holds a pre-computed number.

A tiny worked example — follow this carefully.

Abstract definitions only go so far. Here's the concrete mechanics with a minimal example you can hold in your head.

Suppose the raw patents table looks like this (stripped to the columns that matter for this query):

Raw table — 8 rows (imagine 147 million)
Patent Country Year
EP1000001 EP 2022
EP1000002 EP 2022
EP1000003 EP 2023
US2000001 US 2022
US2000002 US 2022
US2000003 US 2022
JP3000001 JP 2023
JP3000002 JP 2023

Scale this to ~147 million rows. Every query scans them all.

Cube — 4 rows (the pre-computed answer)
SELECT country, year, COUNT(*) AS patents
FROM raw_patents
GROUP BY country, year
Country Year Patents
EP 2022 2
EP 2023 1
US 2022 3
JP 2023 2

Built once. Every dashboard query reads one row from here — not millions from the left.

The raw table (left) has one row per patent. The cube (right) has one row per unique (country, year) combination, with the count already computed. At 8 rows the difference is trivial. At ~147 million rows, the difference is the gap between a dashboard that's instant and one that times out.

The real cube adds more dimensions — company, technology class, family size — and more measures — citation count, forward citations, application-to-grant time. But the mechanics are identical: GROUP BY every dimension combination at build time, store the result, look it up later.

Why it's fast

The cost moved to build time — off the critical path.

Once the cube exists, a dashboard query for "EP filings in 2022" reads one row from the cube table. One row. Not 147 million. The database doesn't need to scan anything — it knows exactly where that row lives.

The pre-aggregation trade-off is explicit and worth understanding:

  • Build time increases. Building the cube means running a large GROUP BY across the whole corpus — that takes real compute. But it happens once, on a big ephemeral machine, while no user is waiting. (More on the build vs serve split in D2.)
  • Storage increases slightly. The cube is a second table you're persisting. It's much smaller than the raw corpus — you're storing aggregated totals, not individual records — but it does take space.
  • Query time drops dramatically. Every dashboard load, every filter change, every leaderboard render is now a narrow key lookup. The user never sees the cost of the GROUP BY.

This is the classic build-time vs read-time trade-off. You can either pay the cost every time you answer the question, or pay it once when you build the cube and then answer for free every time after. For a dashboard that the same question is asked on hundreds of times a day, paying once is the obvious choice.

The cube is built on DuckDB — an in-process analytics database that reads Parquet directly and is extremely fast at exactly these kinds of columnar GROUP BY operations. It produces the cube as a compact file that the serve box loads and keeps ready for queries.

Three query shapes — three artifacts

One size doesn't fit all. Pick the engine for the question.

The cube is the right tool for aggregate trend questions. But the corpus gets three other kinds of questions, and no single engine is best at all of them. Here are the three query shapes and the artifact built for each:

Query shape 1
"Give me everything about patent X."
You know the patent number. You want the full record.
Artifact
Lean lookup DB
(SQLite)

A compact SQLite file with B-tree indexes; point lookup by patent number (plus patent family and citation edges); fast because an index lets it jump straight to the row (a seek), reading one row instead of scanning millions.

Query shape 2
"Rank every company by filings per year."
Aggregate trend across the whole corpus.
Artifact
The cube
(DuckDB · pre-aggregated)

Pre-aggregated totals across all dimensions. The dashboard reads one pre-computed row instead of scanning the corpus. This is the page you're reading.

Query shape 3
"Find patents about self-healing polymers."
Keyword search across 147 million text fields.
Artifact
Tantivy search index
(Tantivy)

A Tantivy inverted index for full-text keyword search. This is NOT DuckDB — DuckDB's built-in text search was too slow at our scale. Tantivy (a Rust search engine, like Lucene) was built specifically for this query shape.

The lesson here is fundamental: don't force one engine to do all three jobs. Each query shape has a different access pattern, and each artifact is tuned for exactly one of them. Using the cube for keyword search, or the search index for aggregate trends, would be like using a hammer to cut bread. Match the tool to the shape of the question.

The reason search isn't just a DuckDB query deserves a sentence: DuckDB is exceptional at columnar GROUP BY analytics, but its built-in full-text search — scanning across hundreds of millions of text fields to find keyword matches — wasn't fast enough at our corpus size. Tantivy uses an inverted index (the same structure behind every search engine: a map from words to document IDs), so a keyword lookup scans almost nothing. Different data structure; completely different performance profile.

Under the hood — performance

Three ways to beat a full scan. The ideas behind all three artifacts.

The three artifacts above share a common goal: avoid reading more data than necessary to answer a question. Here are the concrete techniques that make that possible, in order from slowest baseline to fastest answer.

🐌
Baseline — the slow path

Full table scan — reading every row.

The simplest way to answer any query is to read every row in the table, check whether it matches, and accumulate results. This is a full table scan. The cost grows directly with the row count: fine at thousands of rows, brutal at ~147 million on every single query. The cube, the lookup index, and data skipping all exist for one reason — to avoid this path.

🔍
Technique 1 — jump instead of scan

Index — O(log n) seek instead of O(n) scan.

A database index is a sorted side-structure (a B-tree) that stores keys in order alongside pointers to the rows that hold them. To find patent EP1234567, the engine walks the B-tree in a handful of steps — O(log n) — and jumps straight to that row. It never reads the other 146,999,999 rows at all. This is exactly how the SQLite lookup answers "patent by number / family / citations" in milliseconds.

The inverted index inside Tantivy is the search-engine cousin of the same idea — instead of a sorted key tree, it's a map from every word to the list of documents containing it. Different structure, same principle: skip what you don't need. Contrast: scanning a 147-million-row phone book cover-to-cover vs. jumping to the alphabetical section — same data, completely different cost.

⚡
Technique 2 — when you must scan, scan fast

Vectorized execution — process columns in batches, not row-by-row.

Aggregates — the cube build, ad-hoc analytics — can't always use an index. They genuinely touch many rows, because the answer requires combining many values. But not all scans are equal. DuckDB uses vectorized execution: instead of processing one row at a time and calling out to each column, it works on a column in batches of thousands of values at once. The CPU stays busy, cache lines are hot, and SIMD instructions can operate on the whole batch in parallel. That is a large part of why a columnar engine scans far faster than a row store would for the same data.

🗺️
Technique 3 — skip whole blocks before reading

Data skipping — "mini cubes" in every Parquet block.

Each block in a Parquet file stores the min and max of every column it contains — a tiny summary called a zone map. When a query has a filter (e.g. year = 2023), the engine reads those zone maps first. Any block whose year range doesn't overlap 2023 is skipped entirely — it's never decompressed, never touched. This is data skipping. Pair it with partition pruning — files organized by year so whole files are skipped — and a query can touch a small fraction of the corpus even without a classic B-tree index.

📦
Technique 4 — read the answer, not the data

Partial pre-aggregation — build cubes for the hot paths only.

You might ask: why not pre-aggregate every possible combination of dimensions? Because the combinations explode combinatorially. Every extra dimension multiplies the cube size — company × year × country × IPC class × family size quickly produces more cells than the original data, most of which are never queried. Build and storage cost blow up.

The practical answer is partial pre-aggregation: build a few smaller cubes covering the dimension sets people actually use — by-year, by-country, by-company — instead of one all-encompassing cube. Pre-aggregate the hot paths; let the rare, ad-hoc queries fall back to a fast vectorized scan over the slim analytics table. Most queries hit a pre-computed row; the outliers pay for a scan that is still fast because of vectorization and data skipping.

Three ways to beat a full scan: don't read what you don't need (indexes + data skipping), read fast when you must (vectorized columnar execution), and read the answer instead of the data (pre-aggregation / cube). Every architectural choice on this page traces back to one of those three.

Building a cube at scale

Shard, don't grow the box. The recurring lesson of big-data builds.

Building a cube sounds like a single SQL statement: GROUP BY country, year, company, .... At small scale that's exactly what it is. At billions-of-rows scale, it becomes an exercise in memory management — and the most common mistake is to treat it as a machine-size problem when it's actually an algorithm problem.

The trap: a naive global aggregate over a very large table tries to hold the intermediate GROUP BY state in RAM all at once. As you add more dimensions and more data, that intermediate state grows. At some point — even on a very large machine — you run out of memory. The instinct is to get a bigger box. But there's no box big enough if you're not changing the approach; there's always a corpus large enough to exhaust it.

The fix is partitioning. Instead of one global aggregate over everything, you split the work into bounded buckets — by patent family, by country, by year, or any axis that keeps each bucket small enough to fit comfortably in memory. Each bucket is aggregated independently. Then the results are combined. The total work is the same; the peak memory at any point is the size of one bucket, not the whole corpus.

This pattern — shard, don't grow the box — is the recurring lesson of any big-data build pipeline. The right mental model is not "how big a machine do I need?" but "what's the right unit of work that fits in bounded memory?" Once you design the build step around that constraint, you can process a corpus of any size on a machine of fixed memory, and adding data means adding more bounded buckets — not a bigger box.

In practice this means per-partition deduplication before the final GROUP BY: deduplicate within each bounded bucket first (each one fits in memory and produces a modest intermediate), then combine the already-deduplicated intermediates. The final combining step is lightweight because you're not re-reading the raw corpus — you're combining small summary tables.

This is not unique to patent data. The same principle applies any time you're building an aggregate over a corpus that exceeds available RAM. Design for bounded memory from the start; retrofitting it later is painful.

Up next: the company name problem. The cube in the example above groups by country and year — clean, unambiguous values. But grouping by company is much harder: "Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd.", "SAMSUNG ELECTRONICS CO LTD", and "Samsung Electronics" are the same company but appear as different strings in the raw data. A cube grouped by company is only as trustworthy as the company names feeding it. That's the problem entity resolution solves — and it's what D4 covers next.
