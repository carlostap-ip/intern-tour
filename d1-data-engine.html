Track II · The data engine · ~6 min

The other half — an analytics engine, not a database.

The app you just toured is one half of the company. The other half ingests the world's patent data and turns it into answers. It's built on completely different foundations — here's why.

Two different jobs

One machine writes. The other counts.

The TAP•IP app you toured in chapters 01–15 is an operational system: one tenant opens their workspace, creates an idea, saves a note. Everything happens one row at a time, one user at a time. Writes are frequent; queries are narrow.

The data engine is a completely different kind of machine. Its questions look like this: "How many patents did every Fortune-500 company file per year for the last decade?" That's an OLAP query — it scans hundreds of millions of records, groups them by company and year, and returns a leaderboard. No single tenant, no narrow row lookup; the whole world's corpus at once.

OLTP · the app
Row at a time.

"Save this idea." "Load my workspace." One user, narrow queries, heavy writes. Postgres is perfect.

OLAP · the engine
Corpus at a time.

"Rank every company by filings, worldwide, 2000–2024." Hundreds of millions of records, world-spanning aggregates. Needs a different machine entirely.

One product note: this is an innovation-analytics engine. Patents are signal #1, but they're one of several signals the platform brings together to paint the full picture of who's innovating and where.

Why not just a big Postgres?

The operational database is tuned for the opposite job.

Postgres stores rows. To answer "patents per company per year," it has to read every row, check every column it needs, and group the results. That's fine for thousands of rows. At hundreds of millions of rows spread across a decade's worth of filings from dozens of countries, it gets slow and expensive fast — you'd need a very large machine running all the time just to keep the index warm.

The deeper problem is shape. Postgres stores each row together on disk — great for fetching "give me everything about patent #X123." But an analytics query only needs two or three columns out of thirty, and it needs them across every record. Row-oriented storage forces you to read the columns you don't need, every single time.

This tension — operational writes vs. analytical reads — is old and well-understood. It motivates a whole different class of architecture: the lakehouse.

Lake → warehouse → lakehouse

Three ideas, one clean solution.

To understand the lakehouse, you need to meet its two parents first.

🏞️
Idea 1

The data lake.

Just store everything as raw files in cheap object storage — an object store like S3 or Cloudflare R2. Flat files, no schema enforced, trivially cheap to write and hold forever. The catch: querying raw files is slow unless you build a lot of extra infrastructure on top.

🏭
Idea 2

The data warehouse.

A purpose-built analytics store (BigQuery, Redshift, Snowflake) that is very fast at OLAP queries. The catch: you must load your data into it on a separate step, pay per query, and often keep a copy of your data in a format the warehouse controls.

✨
Idea 3 — what we use

The lakehouse.

Run warehouse-speed analytics directly on the lake's files — no separate load step, no copy. This works because modern columnar file formats like Parquet store data column-by-column, so an analytics engine can read only the columns it needs and skip everything else. The files live in the object store (cheap, durable, infinitely scalable); the query engine reads them directly at full speed.

Ours: raw Parquet files on Cloudflare R2 (the lake), queried by DuckDB (the engine). No load step. No copy. No warehouse bill.

Parquet is worth a moment: instead of storing a row as one contiguous chunk, it groups all values for the same column together. Ask "sum of filings" and the engine reads one thin strip of the file and skips the rest. At ~147.6M resolved rows that difference is enormous.

The big picture

From patent offices to answers.

Raw patent data arrives from EPO BDDS (free public area, no auth) and USPTO bulk data. It lands in Cloudflare R2 as Parquet files — the lake. A build step uses DuckDB to transform, deduplicate, and pre-aggregate the data into four serving artifacts: a Tantivy full-text search index, a lean SQLite lookup DB, a slim analytics table, and the cube. Those artifacts power the API that the TAP•IP app calls.

Each box gets its own page next. D2 walks the full ingest pipeline — how raw XML from the patent offices becomes clean Parquet on R2. D3 explains what the cube is and why pre-aggregation matters. D4 covers entity resolution — how we figure out that "Samsung Electronics Co., Ltd." and "SAMSUNG ELECTRONICS" are the same company. D5 looks at semantic search — finding patents by meaning, not just keywords. D6 closes the loop on why this whole approach wins over a traditional warehouse at our scale.
